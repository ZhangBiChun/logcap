Deep Temporal Graph Infomax for Imbalanced Insider Threat Detection Peng Gao a,b, Haotian Zhanga,b, Ming Wangc, Weiyong Yanga,b, Xinshen Weia,b, Zhuo Lvd, and Zengzhou Maa,b aNARI Group Corporation/State Grid Electric Power Research Institute, Nanjing, China; bNanjing NARI Information & Communication Technology co. ltd, Nanjing, China; cState Grid Corporation of China, Beijing, China; dState Grid Henan Electric Power Research Institute, Zhengzhou, China ABSTRACT Insider threats pose a significant concern for critical information infrastructures. Graph neural networks are widely used for detection due to their ability to model complex relationships among network entities. However, deep learning algorithms struggle with learning from business system data as anomalies are extremely rare. To tackle this challenge, we propose deep temporal graph infomax (DTGI), a new method for detecting insider threats in real-world scenarios with highly imbalanced data. DTGI utilizes an extended continuous-time dynamic heterogeneous graph network and a behavior context constraint anomaly sample generator. This generator incorporates attack behavior context constraints to augment attack samples and enhance the performance of the supervised model. Extensive experiments conducted on the CERT dataset, consisting of over one million records, demonstrate that DTGI surpasses state-of-the-art methods in terms of detec­ tion performance. KEYWORDS Insider threat; anomaly detection; dynamic graph; graph neural network; graph contrastive learning Introduction In the current era of IoT, with billions of computing devices interconnected globally via cyberspace, cyber risks have become increasingly serious. Particularly, according to the literature,1 insider threats which account for roughly 60% of all cyberattacks and involve malicious actions by users with authorized access to an organization’s information system. Traditional border protection methods are rendered ineffective2–4 by such “approved” malicious activities, leading to problems like privacy revelation and computer system sabotage, and making insider threats a preferred method for high-level attacks. Consequently, many techniques have been developed for anomaly behavior detection, however, most earlier techniques were unable to accurately cap­ ture the multidimensional relationships between attack steps due to the covert attack chain of insider threat, resulting in low detection accuracy. In this paper, we aim to develop a detection model that effectively captures insider threat attacks character­ ized by multistep, light-weight, and long-time interval behaviors. Graphs, as compared to sequences and grids, can better model the diverse interaction behavior among network entities,5 and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as promising approaches for detecting complex network attacks. Continuous-time dynamic heterogeneous graph network (CDHGN) based methods have been proposed to model network behaviors and detect sophisticated attacks.6–8 However, the characteristics of insider threat data, such as large scale, lack of labels, and class imbalance, pose significant challenges to supervised learning methods, as anomalies occur rarely (0.001–1%) and the majority of data is normal. These supervised learning methods still have problems learning from imbalanced data. In the production environment, it is difficult to dis­ tinguish in advance which data in the real system is related to potential attacks. In addition, malicious sam­ ples of insider threats are also difficult to obtain. Manual labeling is working however extremely time-consuming and labor-intensive. The sample imbalance issue is likely to cause problems with the graph neural network model, including overfitting, poor generalization, and weak robustness, which eventually decreases its ability to identify abnormal behaviors. Self-supervised learning is the common technique for addressing this issue. It uses well-designed proxy tasks to mine its own super­ vision information from large-scale unlabeled data, which is then used to train the classification model according to downstream tasks. In order to reduce the over-reliance on labels, self-supervised learning-based approaches realize training on a large amount of unla­ beled data. It is important to note that Rshield,6 CDHGN7 and SCGN8 are all supervised meth­ ods, and they employ a repeated sampling approach to generate negative samples in cases where there is insuf­ ficient negative sample availability. This repeated sam­ pling technique can reduce the generalization performance of the models. Hence, these methods are not suitable for direct comparison with DTGI. Here, we only consider RShield as a representative of this kind of methods in our evaluation.Dataset The CERT dataset used in our experiments consists of event log lines containing labeled insider threat activ­ ities. These activities are generated by sophisticated user models from a simulated organization’s computer net­ work. The dataset comprises five log files that record various computer-based activities of employees within the simulated organization. These activities include logon/log off events, HTTP traffic, e-mail traffic, file operations, and the usage of external storage devices. The CERT dataset encompasses 4,000 users who gener­ ated a total of 135,117,169 events (log records) over a period of 516 days. For our experiments, we randomly sampled 41 users and selected 1,306,644 events from their logs. Among these events, 470 were manually screened and labeled as ground truth by domain experts, which represent five different insider threat scenarios. In addition to event logs, the dataset also includes user attribute metadata. Specifically, there are six categorical attributes: role, project, functional unit, department, team, and supervisor. These attributes provide addi­ tional context about the users involved in the activities. In the CERT (V6.2) dataset, each of the five attack scenarios consists of a series of attack steps performed by a single malicious user. This poses a challenge for using supervised detection methods. The original data stores logs of insider activities in five separate files: login/log out, removable media, HTTP, e-mail, and file operations. To facilitate analysis and feature extraction, we integrate the heterogeneous log information from the five separate files into a homogeneous file and per­ formed feature extraction for insider malicious beha­ vior. We extract two types of information as data features: attribute features and statistical features. Attribute features encompass the user attribute meta­ data mentioned earlier, along with additional details such as e-mail addresses, specific behaviors, and time­ stamps associated with the events. Statistical features capture various characteristics related to insider mali­ cious behavior. These include indicators such as logging in or using portable devices outside of working hours, leaving the job within a two-month period, accessing suspicious websites like “wikileaks.org,” and logging into other users’ accounts. Experiment setup We implemented a prototype of DTGI which was devel­ oped using Python 3.8.5, and the PyTorch 1.11.0. The experiments were conducted on a PC workstation with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Silver 4210 CPU @ 2.20 GHz and 256GB RAM. The operating system used was Ubuntu 18.04 64-bit. The GPU used was an Nvidia GV100 with 32GB VRAM. The model parameters are initialized with a gaussian distribution and updated and optimized using the Adam optimizer.38 The parameter settings are as follows: a learning rate of 0.005, a regularization coefficient of 0.001, and a random dropout ratio of 0.6. In this experi­ ment, an early stopping strategy is employed to prevent overfitting. Specifically, if the validation loss does not decrease for 100 iterations, the training is stopped. The hyperparameters of DTGI are adjusted based on the vali­ dation set. To ensure fairness, all semi-supervised contras­ tive learning algorithms use exactly the same data partitioning. Additionally, the learned node representa­ tions for all algorithms are set to be 128-dimensional. Performance metrics We employ precision, recall, and F1 score as the pri­ mary performance metrics. For an anomaly detection problem, if we divide the detection results into posi­ tive class (Positive) and negative class (Negative), there are four possible classification outcomes for the anomaly detection model: TP (True Positive), cor­ rectly predicted positive instances where an event is normal behavior and is also classified as normal beha­ vior; FN (False Negative), incorrectly predicted nega­ tive instances where an event is normal behavior but classified as anomalous behavior; FP (False Positive), incorrectly predicted positive instances where an event is anomalous behavior but classified as normal beha­ vior; TN (True Negative), correctly predicted negative instances where an event is anomalous behavior and is also classified as anomalous behavior. Recall, also known as sensitivity or true positive rate, represents the proportion of correctly pre­ dicted normal behavior events among all actual normal behavior events, and it is calculated using the following formula: Precision and recall influence each other, and in an ideal scenario, we aim for both values to be high. However, in reality, they often trade off against each other: pursuing high precision may result in low recall, and pursuing high recall may often affect precision. To consider both aspects comprehensively, the F1 score is commonly used: The F1 score can be seen as a weighted average of precision and recall. Its maximum value is 1, the mini­ mum value is 0, and a higher value indicates better model performance. In addition, we also utilize the Area Under the Curve (AUC) as the performance metric to evaluate the detec­ tion results mentioned above. The AUC metric is robust against dataset imbalance, and it quantifies the accuracy of predictions. A higher AUC value, closer to 1, indi­ cates more accurate predictions from the method. Discussion Table 1 provides an overview of the AUC results obtained by the baseline methods and our proposed method DTGI on the CERT dataset. It can be observed that the DTGI achieves the best performance on the CERT dataset. Compared to graph representation learn­ ing algorithms, graph contrastive learning methods based on meta-path erosion functions generally perform better due to the construction of strong negative exam­ ples. From Table 2, it can be seen that on the CERT dataset, DTGI achieves optimal detection results when the negative sample division ratio is set to 226:120:101. It achieves an accuracy of 0.9552, recall of 0.9679, F1 score of 0.9558, and AUC of 0.9632. Table 2 illustrate the detection performance of DTGI on different negative sample divisions of the CERT dataset’s training, validation, and test sets. The results demonstrate the superior detection efficacy of DTGI compared with RShield. When a larger portion of the data is used for training (e.g., a negative sample division ratio of 226:120:101 for training, validation, and test sets), the AUC value reaches 0.9632. Even with a smaller amount of training data (e.g., a division ratio of 68:63:313), our method still achieves an AUC of 0.9581. From Table 2, it also can be seen that the experimental results of DTGI are more stable and less affected by the partitioning of negative samples (mal­ icious activities). This indicates that DTGI exhibits a higher level of robustness and generalization. It is worth noting that the CERT dataset used in our experiments is a well-established and widely used data­ set, also employed by baseline methods in their experi­ ments. Therefore, we are confident that our experiments on this dataset are generalizable and maintain external validity. Overall, graph neural networks can consider both attribute and structural information. DTGI takes into account the historical information of nodes and the information gain from strong negative examples during the information aggregation process, effectively improv­ ing the quality of node representations. The learned node representations by DTGI are more comprehensive and discriminative. Conclusion In this paper, we address the challenge of highly imbal­ anced data in real-world insider threat detection. We propose a new method called DTGI, which leverages extra-long continuous-time dynamic heterogeneous graph networks and a behavior context-constrained anomaly sample generator. Our experimental results demonstrate that the construction of negative samples based on attack behavior context enhances the accuracy of anomaly detection for supervised GNN-based models. Currently, the DTGI method relies on human knowl­ edge of normal behavior and attack behavior to construct negative samples. However, in future work, we aim to explore an automatic approach for constructing attack behavior sequences using threat intelligence information. This would eliminate the manual effort involved in con­ structing negative samples and enable the method to adapt to evolving insider threat behaviors more efficiently. By incorporating threat intelligence and automating the con­ struction of attack behavior sequences, we anticipate further improvements in the effectiveness and efficiency of our DTGI method for insider threat detection. Ltd., Beijing 100844, China) Abstract: [Objective] Modern information systems have become an essential part of enterprise operations. However, these systems are often vulnerable to insider attacks, resulting in frequent security incidents and a heightened risk of data breaches. This has sparked significant interest in insider threat detection among researchers and industry professionals. Existing studies mainly focus on analyzing user activity logs but often overlook the evolving relationships and behavioral patterns among users over time. Furthermore, the common issue of data imbalance affects model performance. [Methods] To address these challenges, this study proposes an insider threat detection model called the DySAT_DNN model, which leverages a dynamic self-attention deep neural network. First, the model uses the CERT R4.2 user behavior log dataset, comprising user behavior logs and organization information. Second, the multisource data is preprocessed by aggregating it on a weekly level to extract numerical features of user behaviors, supported by three key rules designed to construct graph structures. Dynamic graph feature representation is achieved through structural and temporal self-attention layers within the DySAT model. The structural self-attention layer uses an attention mechanism to aggregate neighbor information at individual time points, while the temporal self-attention layer captures evolving behavioral patterns over multiple time points. Finally, a fully connected neural network is used as a classifier, trained to be able to distinguish between normal and abnormal behaviors based on the learned representations. [Results] In this paper, we design four stages to carry out the experimental evaluation: 1) We compare the performance of the DySAT_DNN model with existing classifier models. The detection performance of the DySAT_DNN model in Pmacro, Rmacro, and F1macro are 0.81, 0.80, and 0.81, respectively, which are higher than those of other classifier models; 2) Ablation experiments demonstrated the significant impact of the graph construction rules, with Pmacro improving from 0.65 to 0.81 and Rmacro from 0.67 to 0.80 when all rules were combined, underscoring their importance in enhancing detection performance. Furthermore, the model demonstrated its efficiency and generalizability across datasets, with a computational cost of 235.96 min and Pmacro of 0.89 on the CERT R5.2 validation set; 3) To address the data imbalance issue, an effective sampling strategy was developed to balance the proportion of positive and negative samples; 4) When compared to baseline models, DySAT_DNN achieved a superior AUC of 99.7%, confirming its ability to surpass existing methods. [Conclusions] To tackle the two shortcomings of current insider threat detection research, namely the lack of attention to dynamic user relationships and behavioral patterns, as well as issues of data imbalance, this study proposes an insider threat detection model (DySAT_DNN). Built on a self-attention mechanism, the model dynamically aggregates information from neighboring nodes and captures changes in user behavior over time. The model proposed in this study achieves high detection accuracy, effectively identifying abnormal user activities and enhancing the security of enterprise information systems.
